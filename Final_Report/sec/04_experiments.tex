\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section details the empirical evaluation of our two-stage architecture for fine-grained pet breed recognition. We assess the individual contributions of detection-guided cropping, zero-out masking, class balancing, spatial augmentation, training dynamics, hardware limitations, latency profiling, explainability, and negative results encountered during development.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{subsec:research_questions}

Our evaluation is guided by six primary research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} Does detection-guided cropping improve end-to-end breed recognition compared with classifying the full image?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How much do class weighting, dropout, and spatial augmentations improve macro $F_1$ and minority class recall?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} Does zero-out masking prevent shortcut learning when secondary animals overlap or appear near the primary target?
    \item \textbf{RQ4:} What is gained by ImageNet initialization and extended training schedules relative to early convergence under the same architecture and resolution?
    \item \textbf{RQ5:} How accurately does the complete system reject non-target images while meeting the latency constraint?
    \item \textbf{RQ6:} Do Grad-CAM maps and masking counterfactuals indicate reliance on the animal region or on contextual shortcuts?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dataset Curation and Class Balancing}
\label{subsec:dataset_curation}

Our final dataset consists of 35,423 images, drawing on established benchmarks such as the Oxford-IIIT Pet dataset \cite{parkhi2012catsdogs} and the Stanford Dogs dataset \cite{khosla2011novel}. Initial API data collection provided insufficient density, yielding roughly 12 images per breed \cite{cat_api, thedogapi}. To resolve underrepresentation, we integrated supplementary images from Unsplash \cite{unsplash} and Pixabay \cite{pixabay}, bringing minority breeds to a minimum threshold of 50 images per breed and pushing baseline accuracy past 80\%.

During curation, we addressed the distinction between Tabby and Tiger cat. Rather than merging the categories, we recognized Tiger cat as a fur pattern context and Tabby as the target breed, gathering dedicated image subsets for both. Data verification also identified a complete absence of Dalmatian samples in early downloads; incorporating a dedicated set of 359 Dalmatian images led to a sharp performance gain, raising Dalmatian accuracy from 17\% to 69\% within 10 training epochs.

Keyword scraping for rare breeds introduced semantic noise—such as automated queries for ``Bombay'' cats inadvertently retrieving commercial bottles of Bombay Sapphire Gin—requiring us to strictly filter and restrict dataset expansion to verified sources \cite{unsplash, pixabay}. Furthermore, attempts to synthesize training data for rare breeds using generative image models like DALL-E and Midjourney failed due to significant operational overhead, as manual quality filtering and artifact removal outweighed synthetic expansion benefits.

We partitioned the data into a stratified 80\% training split and a 20\% validation split ($7,085$ images), using perceptual hashing to ensure duplicate or derived images remained strictly within the same split to avoid data leakage.

\paragraph{Class Imbalance Weighting}
To handle uneven sample counts across merged data sources, we calculated dynamic inverse frequency class weights $w_i$:
\begin{equation}
    w_i = \frac{N_{\text{total}}}{C \cdot n_i}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\text{total}}$ represents total training samples, $C=10$ species classes, and $n_i$ class sample counts. Passing this weight vector to \texttt{nn.CrossEntropyLoss} penalizes minority class errors proportionally higher, preventing the network from ignoring rare classes.

\subsection{Training Dynamics, Convergence, and Regularization}
\label{subsec:training_dynamics}

Training models from scratch requires careful monitoring of convergence and overfitting.

\paragraph{Generalization and Overfitting Without Regularization}
Training from uninitialized weights without spatial data transforms leads to rapid memorization. In the unregularized baseline run (Task 7), training accuracy steadily progressed past 87\% for dogs and 58\% for cats. However, validation loss reached its global minimum early (epoch 65 for dogs, epoch 40 for cats) before drifting upwards. Final validation accuracy capped at 78.66\% for dogs and 46.13\% for cats. Without regularizing transformations, convolutional layers optimize for static background artifacts rather than generalized foreground patterns.

\paragraph{Augmentation Efficacy and the Impact of CropMix}
Applying targeted image transformations significantly improves generalization across both sub-datasets:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gaussian Blur (Task 3):} Attenuates high-frequency texture noise, elevating validation accuracy to 82.96\% for dogs and 47.13\% for cats.
    \item \textbf{Horizontal Mirroring (Task 4):} Enforces spatial invariance, pushing validation accuracy to 84.47\% for dogs and 49.89\% for cats.
    \item \textbf{CropMix (Task 5):} Mixing image regions acts as a strong spatial regularizer. For dogs, CropMix increases validation accuracy to 84.99\% while completely flattening the validation loss curve over 50 epochs.
    \item \textbf{Combined Transformations (Task 6):} Applying all transformations simultaneously yields 85.02\% accuracy on dogs.
\end{itemize}

Comparing Task 5 (84.99\%) with Task 6 (85.02\%) demonstrates that CropMix accounts for virtually all performance improvements on the dog classification task, making additional filters like blurring largely redundant.

\paragraph{Convergence Horizon and Epoch Sweeps}
Primary feature learning converged within the first 25 epochs following initial training. Sweeps over 100, 1000, and 3000 epochs provided no measurable validation accuracy gains; instead, extended training caused validation performance to plateau while training accuracy approached 100\%.

\paragraph{Dropout and Augmentation Saturation}
Setting the dropout rate in the classifier head to 0.5 helped prevent co-adaptation. Spatial augmentations including random flipping, rotation, mirroring, and custom \texttt{CropMix} patch mixing \cite{yun2019cutmix} yielded noticeable gains initially. However, performance gains saturated once effective sample density reached roughly 400 images per breed, showing diminishing returns beyond that level. Conversely, applying Gaussian blur as a spatial augmentation degraded classification performance by obscuring fine morphological cues like coat patterns and eye structures.

\paragraph{Train-Validation Divergence Control}
To prevent training and validation accuracy from diverging, we combined Model Checkpointing with Early Stopping \cite{prechelt1998early}. Training stopped if validation accuracy failed to improve for 10 consecutive epochs or if the gap between training and validation accuracy exceeded 7\%.

\subsection{Reject Behavior, Overlap Masking, and Explainability}
\label{subsec:reject_behavior_xai}

\paragraph{Reject Class and Routing}
Images are rejected strictly at the detector routing stage. If YOLOv6 fails to locate a valid cat or dog bounding box or returns a confidence score below the detection threshold, the frame is assigned to the global reject class ($-1$) \cite{li2022yolov6}.

\paragraph{Zero-Out Masking for Overlapping Animals}
To address overlapping animals (RQ3), zero-out masking is applied to secondary bounding boxes. The algorithm evaluates secondary animal spatial areas: if a secondary detected animal occupies more than 40\% of the crop area, masking is bypassed to preserve target animal anatomical structures. When secondary detections occupy 40\% or less of the crop, secondary pixel regions are zeroed out to $[0,0,0]$, suppressing feature leakage.

\paragraph{Explainable AI Analysis}
We applied Grad-CAM \cite{selvaraju2017grad} to the final convolutional feature block of EfficientNetV2-S \cite{tan2021efficientnetv2} using standard $224 \times 224$ input transforms (RQ6). Diagnostic heatmaps confirmed consistent focus on discriminative morphological cues such as ears, eyes, snouts, and coat textures, while full-image baselines frequently exhibited shortcut learning toward background elements \cite{geirhos2020shortcut}.

\subsection{Efficiency Analysis and Latency}
\label{subsec:efficiency}

To answer RQ5 regarding operational latency, execution timing was measured over 1,000 warm iterations with explicit CUDA synchronization:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Ingestion and conversion to $224 \times 224$: $8.12\text{ ms}$
    \item YOLOv6 detection and bounding box extraction: $112.45\text{ ms}$ \cite{li2022yolov6}
    \item Area check and zero-out masking: $14.30\text{ ms}$
    \item EfficientNetV2-S forward pass: $65.76\text{ ms}$ \cite{tan2021efficientnetv2}
\end{enumerate}
Mean warm end-to-end pipeline latency measured $200.63\text{ ms}$ per image, satisfying the $5.0\text{ s}$ operational budget by consuming only $4.01\%$ of allowed time.

\subsection{Hardware Limitations, Bottlenecks, and Negative Results}
\label{subsec:failure_analysis}

Documenting exploratory approaches and constraints provides practical insights into training dynamics and system engineering:

\paragraph{VRAM Footprint and Batch Size Scaling}
Testing input resolutions of $384 \times 384$ pixels caused memory bottlenecks because GPU memory scales quadratically ($O(H \times W)$). A $384 \times 384$ crop contains 147,456 pixels per channel compared to 50,176 pixels at $224 \times 224$ ($2.94\times$ increase). With batch size 32, activation maps exceeded the 7.6 GB VRAM limit on shared nodes, causing out-of-memory errors. Standardizing resolution to $224 \times 224$, reducing batch size to 8, and enabling Automatic Mixed Precision (\texttt{torch.cuda.amp} FP16) \cite{paszke2019pytorch} stabilized memory usage. Sequential training phases required explicit memory clearing via \texttt{del model}, \texttt{gc.collect()}, and \texttt{torch.cuda.empty\_cache()}.

\paragraph{Disk Caching and Pre-Validation}
Executing YOLOv6 inside training loops for every image across every epoch formed a severe bottleneck \cite{li2022yolov6}. We established an automated disk caching system (\texttt{cropped\_cache\_cat} and \texttt{cropped\_cache\_dog}) storing pre-extracted crops. Furthermore, a pre-validation script verifying image integrity and bounding box coordinates prevented SLURM array job crashes.

\paragraph{PyTorch Serialization and SLURM Automation}
Updated PyTorch versions (2.6+) restricted checkpoint loading for complex metadata dictionaries; we resolved this by applying a targeted patch to \texttt{torch.load} setting \texttt{weights\_only=False} \cite{paszke2019pytorch}. Manual hyperparameter tuning was replaced by automated SLURM array scripts. Training from scratch was highly sensitive to learning rate selection; Cosine Annealing ($\eta_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{-3} \to \eta_{\text{min}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$) provided necessary optimization momentum.

\paragraph{Web Scraping Noise}
Keyword scraping for rare breeds introduced semantic noise (e.g., queries for "Bombay" cats retrieved bottles of Bombay Sapphire Gin), requiring us to restrict dataset expansion to verified sources \cite{unsplash, pixabay}.
